Screened, Ed Opt, audition, SHSAT, open — five admissions methods, and each one asks for something different from your child. A single school can use three of them at once, in three different programs, with three different deadlines.
The information exists. It's just scattered across a 600-page directory PDF, the MySchools portal, individual school sites, and a Facebook thread from someone who did this two years ago. Families lose spots to mistakes that were entirely avoidable.
How it works
01 Say what matters to you Set the boring parts as filters — borough, admissions method, school size. Or just write it the way you'd say it to a friend: “strong CS and a soccer team, small classes, nothing over 45 minutes.” Whatever the ask adds, you see as a chip you can take straight back off.
02 Get a list you can act on Every school comes with the things you'd otherwise hunt for one by one: which method its programs use, what the application actually requires, test and audition dates, applicants per seat, and a link to that school's own official listing.
03 Put it in order and take it with you Save the ones you want, drag them into the order you actually mean, and export the ranked list with each program's code — the sheet you keep open in one window while you type into MySchools in the other.

Why you can trust the numbers
Every figure is a value the DOE published, shown as published. Nothing is rounded into a band, re-described in adjectives, or averaged into an index.
Every requirement carries a link to that school's official listing. Requirements change year to year, and ours is a copy — so we always show you where to confirm it.
Missing data is said out loud. Where the DOE doesn't publish a figure you'll read Not reported — never a zero, never a dash that you have to interpret.
And what it will never do
Admit Day does not predict whether your child will get in. There is no chance-of-admission percentage, no reach / target / likely, no fit score, no rating of any school.
Other tools will give you a number. Nobody can actually compute it — the inputs aren't public, and a confident-looking estimate is how families talk themselves out of applying to a school they should have ranked. You'll get 4.1 applicants per seat and the requirements. What that means for your child is your call, not a model's.
Where the data comes from

Some DOE figures are older than the current cycle — survey and academic scores in particular. Those are labelled with their own date wherever they appear, rather than presented as current. We'd rather show you a dated number honestly than a fresh-looking one we can't stand behind.
